# Receive-path probe on the Pi: does the Linux receive path keep a packet train's spacing? (2026-11-27)

**An emulation with `tc netem` on two Raspberry Pi 5** (netns + GRE tap; sender `wtc_sta` on rpi-1, receiver `wtc_srv` on rpi-0; the Pis' real `eth0`/`wlan0` untouched; Pis left clean). Tool: `train_probe.c` / `train_probe_rig.sh` from `ab/algo-select` **8c53a92a** (full hash `8c53a92ac3a755b434db99ebf9602921a9cd58fd`, used through a detached worktree because the commit is not on origin); built on each Pi (`train_probe_rig.sh build`). No library change. Raw results and the rig helpers: branch `ab/train-probe-results`, `examples/perf_hil/experiments/train_probe_results/`.
**Question:** can a receiver measure the first-to-last span of a 10-20 datagram back-to-back train within about 10-20 % at 4 / 30 / 100 / 200 Mbit/s, with which receive mode, and does the HAL path (offload on) hide the spacing?

## 1. Setup and what had to be fixed to get a valid measurement
- **Link:** netem on both taps, one-way delay 5 ms, no loss, `limit 1000`, rate 4000 / 30000 / 100000 / 200000 kbit/s set before each rate (`train_rig.sh rate K`: the qdisc is deleted and added, see below). Overhead 42 B per datagram (IP 20 + UDP 8 + Ethernet 14) is what netem counts: the calibration earlier carried 29.73 Mbit/s of IP bytes at a 30 Mbit/s setting (30 x 1500 / 1514). Spacing check on the sender's `eth0` (outer GRE packets, two per datagram): gaps of 397-400 us at 30 Mbit/s (ideal 402), 3,015 us at 4 Mbit/s (3,020), and **54-56 us at 200 Mbit/s (ideal 60.4)**; at 200 Mbit/s the first inter-datagram gaps of a train were also shorter (15, 28 us) before settling at 55 us.
- **Datagrams:** 1468 B payload (a large sample's fragment), trains of N = 10, 20, 40, 200 trains per cell (first look: 50), one train per `period` (at least twice the train time) so trains never overlap.
- **Receiver modes (7):** the HAL (`tt_receive` / `tt_try_receive`) with `TT_UDP_OFFLOAD` 0 and 1, each with and without `--drain`; a plain socket by `each` (recvmsg per datagram, user stamp plus the kernel's software timestamp), `batch` (recvmmsg, 32) and `each-gro` (UDP_GRO).
- **Sender: plain (one sendto per datagram), not the HAL's GSO.** With a GSO sender (`UDP_SEGMENT`) netem treats a whole train as one packet and releases it as one burst, which is not a shaped link (the first looks, run with the HAL sender, showed every train compressed to about 1 % of the ideal span). That is an artefact of shaping a GSO flow in a software qdisc, not of the receive path; a real bottleneck serialises each segment. The HAL sender through netem was therefore not measured.
- **Two rig mistakes found and fixed before the sweep:** (1) `tc qdisc change` keeps an earlier `slot` setting, so the first pretests (run just after a slot test) had a 4 ms slot active and made everything look compressed; `train_rig.sh` now deletes and re-adds the qdisc for every rate; (2) the probe's rig script read the receiver's port from its output file before the backgrounded receiver had created it, which aborted the first full 200 Mbit/s cells (a one-line `: > "$out"` fix in `train_probe_rig.sh`); the 200 Mbit/s cells were re-run alone (`full200`, 21 cells, 200 trains). The 4/30/100 Mbit/s cells of the first full run and the 200 Mbit/s re-run make the 84-cell full set; the 50-train first look (84 cells) is kept as a cross-check.
- **How to read the probe's output:** the line "datagrams per distinct stamp" prints the number of distinct stamps per train (stamps within 10 us of a group's first are one read): **N = every datagram has its own stamp, 1 = the whole train under one stamp.** The text after it in the output ("n = the whole train under one stamp") and the comment in the source state it the other way round; the data are consistent with the number of stamps (at 4 Mbit/s every mode prints N with a span of 0.999 x ideal, which is only possible if every datagram has its own stamp). In the tables below it is `st` = distinct stamps per train.
- **Warnings that appeared, and whether they matter:** (a) "receive buffer is 425984 bytes (asked 1048576)": `net.core.rmem_max` is 212,992 on the Pis, the HAL's request is capped at twice that; a 40-datagram train is about 60-80 KB, and **`socket_drops` is 0 in every cell of every set and every train arrived whole**, so it did not affect the result. (b) "IORING_REGISTER_PBUF_RING refused a ring (Invalid argument)": it appears in 24 + 19 + 16 files, all `--drain` cells; the drain then does not run as designed (the HAL's `drain_datagrams` counter is 0-27 for hundreds of datagrams), so **the `--drain` rows are effectively the non-drain path and do not test the io_uring drain**; the cause of the refusal was not investigated here. The drain rows' close agreement with the non-drain rows must not be read as a result for the io_uring drain.

## 3. Slotted link (netem `slot 4ms 4ms bytes R/2`: releases up to a rate-times-4-ms byte budget every 4 ms; one set, 100 trains, N = 10 and 20)
The ideal span does not apply to a slotted link (the train leaves in slot bursts), so the figures are not errors of the receive path. They show that **every receive mode reports what the link did**: the user span and the kernel span agree in every cell (e.g. N = 10: 0.59 / 0.59 at 4 Mbit/s, 0.01 / 0.01 at 30, 0.03 / 0.03 at 100); the datagrams of a slot arrive within about 40 us of each other (0.00-0.07 of the ideal span at 30-200 Mbit/s for N = 10), and a train estimator reading bytes over that span would read the slot burst's line rate, not the link's average rate. Slot rows: `slot_tables.txt`, raw files `slot/`.

## 4. Findings
1. **At 4, 30 and 100 Mbit/s every receive mode, including the HAL path with or without receive offload, measures the train span at 0.99-1.00 of the ideal** (p5-p95 0.93-1.04 for the 100 Mbit/s `each` / `batch` / `each-gro`, 0.98-1.00 for the HAL rows), for N = 10, 20 and 40, with **every datagram carrying its own stamp** (`st` = N). The HAL path therefore does **not** hide the spacing, with offload on (`gro_enables=1`) or off. The kernel timestamp (`each`, `each-gro`) equals the user stamp's span (k 1.00); the user stamp lags the kernel's by 30-40 us (last datagram), constant, so it cancels in a span.
2. **At 200 Mbit/s the span reads low by a roughly constant ~55 us:** 0.88-0.91 for N = 10 (p5 down to 0.77-0.86), 0.94-0.96 for N = 20, 0.97-0.98 for N = 40, the same for the kernel stamps (k 0.90 / 0.95 / 0.98), so the kernel timestamp does **not** restore the missing 10 %: the deficit is upstream of the receiver. It matches the shaper: at 200 Mbit/s netem released the datagrams 54-56 us apart instead of 60.4 us and the first two gaps of a train were 15 and 28 us (outer capture on the sender). I take the 200 Mbit/s shortfall to be a property of this rig's shaper (and the train start), not of the Linux receive path; it was not separated further. At this rate the reads group datagrams (distinct stamps per 10-datagram train: 6-9 for the HAL and the plain socket, 5 for `batch`, 20-datagram trains 10-18, 40-datagram trains 22-37), but the first-to-last span is still right to within the shaper's own error.
3. **`batch` (recvmmsg)** has the widest spread at 100 and 200 Mbit/s (p5-p95 0.94-1.04 at 100, 0.77-1.00 at 200 for N = 10) because one user stamp per call covers several datagrams; its median is correct.
4. The `--drain` modes are not evidence for the io_uring drain (PBUF_RING refused, section 1).

## 5. Verdict
**Yes. On the Pi a receiver can measure the first-to-last span of a 10-20 datagram train within about 5 % at 4, 30 and 100 Mbit/s with any of the seven modes, in particular with the HAL's own `tt_receive` / `tt_try_receive` stamps, with receive offload on or off, and without kernel timestamps.** At 200 Mbit/s the same modes read 6 % (N = 20) to 12 % (N = 10) low on this rig, which is inside a 10-20 % tolerance for N >= 20 and at its edge for N = 10 (p5 down to 0.77); the kernel timestamp does not improve it, so the HAL needs nothing extra for this measurement. Limits: one sender kind (plain sendto, no GSO), a software shaper (netem), one flow, 5 ms delay and no loss; the io_uring drain was not exercised (PBUF_RING refused); a slotted link makes any receiver see the slot burst, correctly. ## 6. Addendum 2026-11-28: the sender is the HAL's batch send path (`SEND_HOW=hal`, `tt_send_batch`), under netem and under CAKE
Same rig, rates, train sizes and receiver modes as sections 1-5, 100 trains per cell, 84 cells per shaper (`halsend_netem`, `halsend_cake`; raw files `raw/halsend_*`). The sender is the HAL's own: `tt_send_batch()` = `sendmmsg()`, and `UDP_SEGMENT` (GSO) when `TT_UDP_OFFLOAD` is 1. In the rig script the `hal_offload0*` cells send with offload 0 (sendmmsg, MTU-sized packets one by one into the qdisc) and the `hal_offload1*`, `each`, `batch` and `each_gro` cells with offload 1 (GSO).
**Mechanism (capture on the sender's `eth0`, outer GRE packets, gaps in us, 30 Mbit/s, 10 datagrams, ideal 402 us per datagram):**
| shaper | HAL sender | gaps |
|---|---|---|
| netem | offload 0 (sendmmsg, no GSO) | 377, 396, 398, 400, 394, 396, 399 ... (spaced) |
| netem | offload 1 (UDP_SEGMENT, GSO) | 11, 4, 7, 5, 4, 4, ... (**the whole train in about 100 us: released as one burst**) |
| CAKE (split-gso) | offload 0 | 377, 396, 396, 399, 396 ... (spaced) |
| CAKE (split-gso) | offload 1 (GSO) | 365, 393, 398, 397, 397 ... (spaced) |
| CAKE | plain sendto | 382, 393, 396, 397 ... (spaced) |
**netem does not segment a GSO packet before it applies the rate**: the whole GSO train is one packet to it, delayed by its total size and then handed on as one skb, which the tunnel device segments on the wire at line rate. CAKE splits a GSO packet into its segments first (`split-gso`) and shapes each, so the wire spacing is the shaper's.

| HAL **offload 1 (GSO) under netem** (HAL receiver offload 1, `each`, `batch`, `each-gro`) | **0.00-0.01 / 0.00-0.01 / 0.00-0.09 / 0.03-0.17** | 0.00 / 0.00-0.02 / 0.01-0.07 / 0.03-0.14 | 0.00 / 0.01-0.03 / 0.03-0.09 / 0.08-0.18 |

Kernel stamps agree with the user stamps in every cell (`k`), the stamp counts are as in section 4 (every datagram its own stamp up to 100 Mbit/s, grouped at 200 Mbit/s), and the deficit at 200 Mbit/s has the same size as with the plain sender (0.86-0.90 for N = 10, 0.93-0.95 for 20, 0.96-0.98 for 40). `socket_drops` is 0 in every cell; under netem every train arrived whole, under CAKE one train in each of three 4 Mbit/s cells (99 of 100: a drop in the shaper's queue) did not.
**Answers:** (1) A train sent through `tt_send_batch` **with GSO** reaches the receiver as a burst (span about 0-18 % of the ideal, never within 20 %) **only because netem releases a GSO packet as one unit; that is a rig artefact.** With segmentation before the shaper (CAKE `split-gso`), or with the sender's GSO off (`TT_UDP_OFFLOAD=0`: sendmmsg), the HAL sender's train arrives with the shaper's spacing at 4-100 Mbit/s (span / ideal 0.98-1.00 for every receiver mode) and 0.86-0.98 at 200 Mbit/s (the same shortfall as with a plain sender: the shaper, not the sender). (2) **The sender does not coalesce or space the head**: sendmmsg without GSO and CAKE with GSO both give the ideal spacing. (3) **Which result applies to a real Wi-Fi / 5G link:** a real NIC (or the driver) segments a GSO packet after the qdisc, back to back at the NIC's line rate, and the air then serialises each frame, so the receiver sees the radio's spacing whether or not the sender used GSO: the results with segmentation before the shaper (CAKE rows, and the sendmmsg rows under netem) are the ones that apply; the netem + GSO rows (span ~0) describe the shaper's limitation, not a Wi-Fi/5G behaviour. A Wi-Fi link that aggregates frames (A-MPDU) or a slotted link compresses a train by itself, which the 4 ms slot set in section 3 shows and the receiver reports correctly.
**For the Pi benchmark of the packet-train implementation (decided from these rows):** keep netem, as in every baseline, and send the TickLE arms with **`TT_UDP_OFFLOAD=0`** (already what `wtc_run.sh` sets), so that the HAL sender's train reaches the shaper as MTU-sized packets and the link behaves identically for the new arms, the ADAPTIVE baseline, the fixed rate and the DDS stacks; CAKE would also work but would change the baselines' link behaviour (queue, AQM, no delay or loss), so the baselines would have to be rerun. Not changed: no library, wire, API, default, merge or tag here.
